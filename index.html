Where do you want to start?

If you think you may have a problem

  1. Know that asking is a strong move. Addiction is a health condition, not a character flaw, and it is treatable.
  2. Talk to one safe person. A parent, school counselor, coach, doctor, or older sibling. If that feels impossible, start with a free helpline above.
  3. Get a check-up. A doctor or school nurse can screen for substance use. In most places, teens can talk about this privately, though rules on confidentiality vary by state and situation.
  4. Explore support that fits you. Counseling, outpatient programs, and peer groups all work for different people. See Find support.
  5. If you slip, keep going. Relapse is common and does not mean you failed.

If you’re worried about a friend

  1. Pick a calm, private moment. Not when they are high or drunk.
  2. Start with care, not blame. Try: “I’ve noticed you seem different lately, and I’m worried about you.”
  3. Listen more than you talk. Skip labels like “addict” and avoid ultimatums.
  4. Encourage them to talk to a trusted adult or helpline, and offer to go with them.
  5. Don’t cover for them or keep dangerous secrets. If their life is at risk, tell an adult. That is not betrayal.
  6. Look after yourself too. You can’t fix this alone. See Helping a friend.

If you’re a family member

  1. Stay calm and keep the door open. Shame and anger usually push people away from help.
  2. Ask open questions and listen without lecturing.
  3. Call the SAMHSA helpline (1-800-662-4357) or your pediatrician for guidance and referrals.
  4. Ask about treatment designed for teens. Adolescents often need different approaches than adults.
  5. Find support for yourself, such as Al-Anon/Alateen or Partnership to End Addiction’s parent helpline.

Three addictions we focus on

Addiction means you keep doing something even though it’s hurting you, and stopping feels really hard. It isn’t only drugs and alcohol. These three show up most in teen life.

16.8%of Americans over 12 struggle with addiction to alcohol or drugs alone
20.8%of high school students vaped in 2019, up from 1.5% in 2011 (Meehan et al.)
70%of young people check social media several times a day (Lee et al.)
Vaping

Bright designs and fun flavors are chosen to attract young people, whose brains are still developing.

  • Short term: a slight buzz and a social experience with friends
  • Long term: lung problems, constant cravings, and a lot of money spent
Electronics

Apps are built to keep you looking. Feeds show you more of what you already liked, so it’s hard to stop.

  • Short term: small dopamine hits that make you want to keep scrolling
  • Long term: eye strain and feeling more distant from people in real life
Social validation

Likes, comments, and approval can start to shape how you see yourself.

  • Short term: a small dopamine hit when people respond to you
  • Long term: craving acceptance all the time, linked to anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem (Gaba)

Why it happens: we’re social creatures

We copy each other

You see a friend vaping or posting something that gets attention, and you want to belong. Doing it once or twice can turn into an everyday need. Researchers like Professor Tang point out that people seek the respect and approval of others.

The reward pathway

These habits trigger the reward pathway in your brain. You get a rush, then keep searching for it again.

It’s designed to hook you

Colorful packaging, flavors, flashing signs, and loud sounds on devices are built to catch the attention of young people, whose brains are still developing.

It can happen to anyone

Younger people can get hooked faster, but no age group is safe. It is not a sign of weakness.

If you try to quit, it can feel rough

Withdrawal happens when your body has gotten used to a substance and you stop. It’s a sign your body is adjusting, not a sign you’re failing.

In the body

  • Sweating and chills
  • Nausea
  • Changes in heart rate

In mood and focus (nicotine)

  • Anger, anxiety, or depression
  • Strong cravings
  • Trouble concentrating
  • Impatience, restlessness, and trouble sleeping

Preventing addiction is easier than quitting, but quitting is possible. A doctor, school nurse, or the Smokefree Teen tools can help you plan it.

What helps

Learn it

When you understand why addiction happens, you can spot it early in yourself or a friend, and respond with care instead of judgment.

Make it a community issue

When addiction is treated as something we deal with together, not a personal failure, it removes the shame that keeps people quiet.

Offer better options

In the famous “Rat Park” experiment, animals with richer surroundings and company steered away from drugs. For people, that means real alternatives like teen centers, reading clubs, recreational sports leagues, art, volunteering, and friends to be around.

Talk about it openly

School assemblies, faith communities, and one-on-one conversations with resources on hand all help. Talk about addiction as something many people struggle with, not as one person’s flaw.

Small things to try this week

  • Turn off notifications for one app that pulls you in.
  • Pick one hour a day without your phone, and do something with a friend instead.
  • Tell one person you trust what you’re noticing. That’s it for step one.
  • If you vape, text or call a helpline below. You can ask a question without committing to anything.

Helping a friend, and staying okay yourself

Know the overdose signs

  • Can’t be woken up or is unresponsive
  • Slow, shallow, or no breathing
  • Blue or gray lips and fingertips
  • Choking or gurgling sounds

Call 911. Many states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help. Ask your local health department about naloxone (Narcan), a medicine that can reverse an opioid overdose.

Things you can say

  • “I care about you. I’m here whenever you want to talk.”
  • “You don’t have to do this alone.”
  • “Can I help you find someone to talk to?”

Protect your own health

  • You’re not responsible for their choices.
  • Set boundaries and say no to using with them.
  • Talk to a counselor or join Alateen.

Common questions

Will I get in trouble if I ask for help?

Helplines are confidential and are there to support you, not punish you. Rules at schools and clinics vary, so you can ask “Is this confidential?” before sharing.

Does needing help mean I’m addicted?

Not necessarily. Substance use exists on a spectrum, and early support can prevent bigger problems. A professional can help you figure out where you are.

Can I quit on my own?

Some people can, but stopping certain substances suddenly, like alcohol or opioids, can be dangerous. Talk to a doctor first.

What if I relapse?

Relapse is common in recovery and doesn’t erase your progress. Reach out for support and try again.

How do I pay for treatment?

Many programs accept insurance or Medicaid, and some offer sliding-scale fees or free services. The SAMHSA helpline can point you to options.
